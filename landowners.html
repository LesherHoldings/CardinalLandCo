Four ways your land can pay you

Solar, natural gas, minerals, and timber. What each one is, how the money tends to flow, and the questions to ask before you sign anything.

Solar leasing

Solar leases

A solar company pays rent to put panels on your open ground, usually for decades, and the land comes out of crops for the life of the lease.

What it is

Solar developers build large arrays that sell power onto the grid. They look for open, fairly flat ground with few trees and good drainage, close to a substation or a three-phase power line. Fields that have been farmed for generations are often exactly what they want. That is why so many Pennsylvania farmers are getting letters.

Most solar leases begin with an option or development period. The company pays a smaller amount to hold the land while it studies the site, lines up a grid connection, and applies for permits. That stage can take years, and many projects never get built. If the project goes ahead, the lease moves into an operating term that often runs 25 years or more, with options to extend it further. Rent is usually figured by the acre and paid each year. Whether it rises over time depends on what the lease says.

The land comes out of crops for the life of the lease. Farmland enrolled in Pennsylvania’s Clean and Green program can face roll-back taxes when its use changes, and the lease should say who pays them. At the end of the term the company is supposed to remove the panels and restore the ground. How that is guaranteed, and who is responsible if the original company is long gone, is one of the most important things in the document.

A solar lease is a long commitment. It will outlast most mortgages and a good many of the people who sign it. It deserves a slow read and your own attorney.

How we help

We look at your ground the way a developer would: acreage, slope, tree cover, distance to a substation or a three-phase line, and what is being built in your part of the state. Then we tell you plainly whether your land fits. If it does, we introduce you to solar companies we have checked out. They pay our fee, not you. Your own attorney reviews the lease before you sign anything.

Questions to ask before signing

  • How long is the option or development period, what is paid during it, and what happens if the project is never built?
  • How long is the operating term, how many times can the company extend it, and who decides?
  • How is rent figured, when is it paid, and does it change over the years?
  • Who pays the Clean and Green roll-back taxes if the land comes out of the program?
  • Can the company assign or sell the lease to another company, and do you have any say in it?
  • What happens at the end: who removes the panels, who restores the ground, and is there a bond or escrow set aside to pay for it?
  • Which acres are tied up, and which stay yours to farm, hunt, or build on?
  • Who maintains fences, gates, access roads, and drainage, and who pays when they are damaged?
  • Who carries the insurance, and who is liable if someone is hurt on the site?

Natural gas leasing

Natural gas leases

Where the Marcellus and Utica shale lie beneath Pennsylvania, a company may pay a bonus to lease the gas under your land and a royalty on what it produces.

What it is

Gas companies lease the right to drill for and produce natural gas from beneath the land. In Pennsylvania that mostly means the Marcellus and Utica shale, which lie under a large part of the state. A single well pad can draw from many tracts, so when a company moves into a county it often approaches many owners at the same time.

The first question is what you own. In Pennsylvania the oil and gas rights can be separated from the surface, and on many farms they were, generations ago. A family can own and work the land and not own the gas beneath it. The deed and the county records answer that question, and it is worth knowing the answer before anyone sits down at your kitchen table.

A gas lease commonly pays a signing bonus up front and a royalty on whatever is produced. Pennsylvania law sets a minimum royalty for oil and gas leases. The primary term is usually a few years, but once a well is producing, the lease can stay in force for as long as production continues. Old leases on a property can be held that way for decades, which is why it is also worth knowing whether your land is already under one.

What happens on the surface matters as much as what happens below it. Well pads, access roads, pipelines, water lines, and compressor stations all take ground. A lease can limit where those go, or it can leave the company free to choose. Shut-in clauses, pooling with neighboring tracts, and what may be deducted before your royalty is figured are all terms your attorney should read closely.

How we help

We check what the records show you own, which companies are active in your county, and whether your tract is the kind they are leasing now. Then we tell you what we find. If there is real interest, we introduce you to companies we have checked out, and they pay our fee. Your own attorney reviews the lease.

Questions to ask before signing

  • Does the deed say you own the oil and gas rights, or were they severed years ago?
  • What is the signing bonus, when is it paid, and what happens if the company never drills?
  • How is the royalty figured, and what costs may be deducted before you are paid?
  • How long is the primary term, what keeps the lease in force after it ends, and what does the shut-in clause allow?
  • Can your acres be pooled with neighboring tracts, and how does that change what you are paid?
  • Where may well pads, roads, pipelines, and water lines go, and which parts of the farm are off limits?
  • Can the lease be assigned or sold to another company without your consent?
  • How are production and payments reported to you, and do you have the right to audit them?
  • Who repairs damage to fields, fences, drainage, and water supplies, and who carries the insurance?

Mineral rights

Mineral rights

The rights beneath your land can be leased or sold on their own, and many Pennsylvania families are not sure what they hold.

What it is

In Pennsylvania, what lies under a piece of land can be owned separately from the surface. Coal, oil and gas, stone, sand, and gravel can each be severed from the surface and sold, leased, or passed down on their own. A tract that has been in one family for a century may have had its rights split off more than once along the way.

Many landowners simply do not know what they own. The deed may be silent, or it may refer back to an older deed nobody has read in decades. A title search at the county courthouse answers the question. An attorney or a title company can run one, and it is worth doing before you answer any offer.

An offer to buy your mineral rights is a different thing from a lease. A lease ends. A sale is permanent, and it binds your heirs. The companies and individuals who buy mineral rights are often investors rather than operators. They are not planning to drill or mine themselves. They are betting that the rights will be worth more later than they pay you today.

There are honest reasons to sell. Some owners would rather have money now than a royalty that may never come. Others want to settle an estate or clean up a title before the land passes to the next generation. The point is to decide with the facts in hand, not because a letter arrived with a deadline on it.

How we help

We help you find out what the records show you own, who is buying or leasing in your county, and what else is happening in your part of the state. Then we tell you plainly what we find. If you want to go further, we introduce you to buyers and operators we have checked out, and they pay our fee. Your own attorney handles the title work and the papers.

Questions to ask before signing

  • What exactly do the deed and the county records say you own beneath the surface?
  • Is this an offer to lease the rights or to buy them outright, and is the sale permanent?
  • Who is making the offer: an operator that will drill or mine, or an investor that will hold or resell?
  • Which rights are included: all of them, or only certain minerals, depths, or formations?
  • If it is a lease, how long does it run, what holds it in force, and how is the royalty figured?
  • What rights to the surface come with the deal, and can the buyer or a later owner use your land to reach the minerals?
  • How would a sale affect your taxes, your estate plan, and any mortgage or farm program on the land? Your accountant and attorney should answer this one.
  • Is there a deadline in the offer, and what is the reason for it?
  • Has your own attorney read the deed or lease before you sign it?

Timber sales

Timber sales

Standing timber is a crop like any other, and a well-run sale looks nothing like a handshake with the first logger who pulls in the lane.

What it is

The trees in your woodlot are a crop, even if nobody planted them. What they are worth depends on species, size, quality, how easy they are to reach, and what the mills are paying that season. A stand of straight, mature hardwood near a good road is a very different thing from a steep hillside of small or crooked trees. Only someone who has walked the woods can say which you have.

A consulting forester works for you, not for the buyer. The forester inventories the stand, marks the trees to be cut, and runs a competitive sale so that several buyers bid on the same marked timber. The Pennsylvania DCNR Bureau of Forestry also has service foresters who advise landowners at no charge. Either way, a good sale starts with knowing what you have.

Timber sales should be done in writing. The contract should say which trees are included and how they are marked, where the boundaries are, when and how you are paid, how long the buyer has to cut, how the woods are to be left, and who is responsible for damage. Payment is usually either a lump sum before cutting begins or a price per unit as the logs are scaled. Buyers sometimes post a bond or deposit that is held until the job is finished properly.

A walk-up offer from a logger is rarely the only option, and the day it arrives is rarely the day to decide. A reputable buyer will not mind if you take a few weeks to have the stand looked at. One who presses for a signature today is telling you something.

How we help

We look at your woods, your access, and what you want from the land, and we tell you plainly what we see and whether buyers are likely to be interested. If you want to sell, we introduce you to reputable buyers we have checked out, and to consulting foresters who can inventory and mark the stand. The buyers pay our fee, not you. Your own attorney reviews the contract.

Questions to ask before signing

  • Has a consulting forester inventoried the stand and marked the trees to be cut?
  • Which trees are included, how are they marked, and where exactly are the property lines?
  • Is payment a lump sum before cutting begins or pay-as-cut, and if pay-as-cut, who scales the logs and how are the tallies reported to you?
  • Does the buyer post a bond or deposit, and what has to happen before it is returned?
  • How long does the buyer have to finish, and in which seasons will the cutting take place?
  • How will the woods be left: skid trails, log landings, tops, stream crossings, and the condition of your roads and gates?
  • Who is liable for damage to fences, crops, buildings, and neighboring property, and does the buyer carry liability insurance and workers’ compensation?
  • Will a written contract be signed before any equipment comes onto the property?
  • Have you asked the DCNR service forester for your county, or your own attorney, to look the contract over?
